# SUPPLEMENT-TRUTH-01 — research lock

**Audience:** general healthy adults, India-first context.
**Format:** approximately 60 seconds; English.
**Rule:** no supplement is presented as universally essential, curative, or a substitute for food, diagnosis, or prescribed care.

## Claims approved for script and screen

| Topic | Accurate teaching point | Who may benefit / who should not guess | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Protein powder | It is a convenience food, not an essential supplement when a person can meet protein needs from food. | Consider only where food access, appetite, travel, or total dietary intake makes food targets impractical. India’s 2024 dietary guidance cautions against regular protein-powder use. | ICMR-NIN 2024 reporting; NIH ODS exercise fact sheet |
| Vitamin B12 | Natural B12 is limited to animal foods; fortified foods and supplements can reduce deficiency risk where animal foods are excluded. | Prioritise vegan/very-low-animal-food diets; discuss testing/plan with a clinician for older adults, GI disease/surgery, or long-term metformin/acid suppression. | NIH ODS, Vitamin B12 Health Professional Fact Sheet |
| Vitamin D | Blood 25(OH)D is the main status marker; high-dose intake is not a universal wellness intervention. | Discuss testing/clinician plan for limited sun exposure, dark skin, malabsorption, obesity, or known low status. | NIH ODS, Vitamin D Health Professional Fact Sheet |
| Iron | Iron is for diagnosed deficiency or clinician-led pregnancy care, not “energy” guessing. | Higher-risk groups include heavy menstrual bleeding, pregnancy, frequent blood donation, certain GI disorders, and restricted diets. | NIH ODS, Iron Consumer and Pregnancy Health Professional Fact Sheets |
| Creatine | Creatine monohydrate is a well-supported performance option for healthy adults who perform resistance or repeated high-intensity work. | Optional—not essential. Use clinician guidance with kidney disease, pregnancy, medication questions, or other individual contraindications. | ISSN creatine position stand; NIH ODS exercise fact sheet |
| Multivitamins / omega-3 | They can fill documented intake gaps but are not a default disease-prevention shortcut for every adult. | Pick a nutrient and reason; avoid stacking products. For omega-3, food is preferred for most people and treatment-dose decisions belong with a clinician. | NIH ODS multivitamin and omega-3 fact sheets |
| Product risk | Multi-ingredient “fat burners”, “test boosters”, and proprietary blends make dose, interaction, and contamination assessment harder. | Competitive athletes need additional third-party testing / anti-doping review. | IOC consensus statement on supplements |

## Safety language retained in final script

- Do not self-start high-dose vitamin D or iron.
- Pregnancy, kidney/liver disease, and prescription medicines require clinician/pharmacist review.
- “Test or assess the gap” does **not** mean everybody needs a blood panel; it means a real reason precedes a product.
